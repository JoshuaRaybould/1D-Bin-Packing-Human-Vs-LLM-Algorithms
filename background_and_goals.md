# Background

Bin packing is one of the central problems in combinatorial optimisation. It is set up such that we assume we have an infinite number of bins each with some capacity c, and we have some finite number of items n to pack into them. These items each take on a value v ≤ c, this value generally represents the weight or size of the item. Since the maximum value that an item can take is equal to that of the capacity of a bin, we can guarantee that we will be able to fit all the items into the bins. The goal is to assign each item to a bin whilst minimising the number of bins used - in other words, we want to minimise wasted space. When it comes to 1D bin packing (which is what we are handling here), there are two variants. First is the offline problem, where the algorithm being used has access to all items. Second is the online problem, where 1 item is given to the algorithm at a time, at which point it must decide where to place it, meaning it has access to less information. Here, we will be looking at the offline case.

This problem falls into the category NP-Hard and it would take exponential time to arrive at the exact solution. For this reason, it is a good idea to use heuristic or randomised algorithms that whilst don't generally give the exact solution they can come close and importantly do so in reasonable time. Other than being interesting due to its innate difficulty, bin packing also has many real-world applications such as filling up trucks which have a weight capacity and filling up containers among other things. Additionally, problems in scheduling often contain it as a special case.

Here I plan to explore a variety of randomised algorithms that can be applied to this problem, implementing each, then analysing and comparing their performance. Randomised algorithms are those that use random numbers to influence their choices, therefore given a fixed input their output may change. Randomised algorithms can vary drastically in complexity, and the aim here is to cover as large a variety as possible. An extremely basic randomised algorithm could consist of simply randomising the order of the input given to an existing deterministic algorithm. This is useful in the case you suspect the order of the input may be poor for your algorithm, for example if it was produced by an adversary, by randomising the order we can combat this and hope to improve performance. On the other hand, there are many complex algorithms which draw inspiration from evolution and organisms in general; and there are also many cases of people combining multiple algorithms.

The bin packing problem has been studied extensively, and a very large number of algorithms have been proposed and applied to the problem, due to this, it is reasonable to expect that generative AI like ChatGPT will also be able to produce algorithms to solve it when prompted. This provides an opportunity to compare a solution arrived at by a human with access to the literature to that of generative AI. Comparing these will be an additional goal of this project.
