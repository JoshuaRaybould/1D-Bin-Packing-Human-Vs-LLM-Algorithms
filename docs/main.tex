\begin{abstract}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0.6em}
\noindent The one-dimensional offline Bin Packing Problem (BPP) is a strongly NP-hard combinatorial optimisation problem with wide-ranging practical applications. This study addresses three research questions centred on the empirical performance of randomised algorithms for the BPP and the capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate competitive implementations of those algorithms from scratch.

To establish a robust baseline, we implemented, tuned, and evaluated eight stochastic algorithms spanning simple heuristics (Randomised Best Fit), local search methods (Simulated Annealing, Tabu Search, Variable Neighbourhood Search), multi-start approaches (GRASP), population-based evolutionary methods (Grouping Genetic Algorithm), and swarm intelligence (Ant Colony Optimisation, Fitness Dependent Optimizer). All algorithms were benchmarked across custom-generated instance sets ranging from 100 to 1600 items, with solution quality measured against the $L_2$ lower bound of Martello and Toth. Among the human implementations, GGA, VNS, and SA formed a strong top tier, with GGA achieving the best overall solution quality and SA offering the most favourable balance of quality and computational efficiency due to its near-flat runtime scaling.

To evaluate LLM code generation capability, we developed an automated iterative refinement pipeline incorporating correctness validation and performance-driven optimisation, and applied it to two flagship models: OpenAI's GPT-5.2 and Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6. Each model was tasked with generating implementations for all seven non-trivial algorithms under a zero-shot prompting strategy with Chain-of-Thought reasoning. The resulting implementations were evaluated against the human baselines on standard BPPLIB benchmark instances, with each implementation allocated an identical wall-clock time budget.

The results show that both LLMs produced functionally correct code for six of the seven algorithms, deviating from the specified paradigm only on FDO, and frequently matched or exceeded human-level performance. Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 achieved the lowest weighted average deviation from the known optimal on four of the seven algorithms, incorporating design choices absent from the human baselines---notably a dynamic cooling schedule for Simulated Annealing and an iterative bin-reduction formulation for Tabu Search. The human implementations retained an advantage on the Genetic Algorithm and Variable Neighbourhood Search, where the human's choice of bin-level encoding and exhaustive local search respectively outperformed the alternative approaches adopted by both LLMs. OpenAI's GPT-5.2 exhibited higher variance, achieving the strongest result on ACO but struggling on SA due to a fundamental objective function flaw that rendered the search almost entirely uninformative despite producing syntactically correct and executable code. Neither model produced a faithful adaptation of the Fitness Dependent Optimizer, instead defaulting to more familiar algorithmic paradigms. These findings suggest that flagship LLMs are now capable of producing competitive metaheuristic implementations for well-studied combinatorial problems, but remain susceptible to subtle reasoning errors that are difficult to detect through automated correctness testing alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem Background}
The bin packing problem (BPP) is one of the central problems in combinatorial optimisation. In its standard formulation, a set of items with corresponding values (generally size or weight) must be packed into an unlimited supply of bins, each with a uniform capacity. The objective is to assign the items to the bins such that the total number of bins used is minimised. This is the one-dimensional (1D) bin packing problem, which is conceptually similar to the 1D cutting stock problem \cite{maxence}. The 1D BPP is primarily studied in two variants: the offline problem, where the algorithm has access to the weights of all items simultaneously, and the online problem, where items are revealed and packed one at a time. In this study, we focus on the offline case.

The 1D BPP is classified as NP-Hard, meaning that no known polynomial-time algorithm exists for computing an exact solution. Consequently, heuristic and randomised algorithms are often employed to obtain near-optimal solutions efficiently. Beyond its theoretical significance, bin packing has numerous real-world applications, ranging from loading transport vehicles with weight capacities to minimising waste in industrial cutting processes \cite{munien}. Additionally, many complex scheduling problems contain the BPP as a special case.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
Here we formalise the classical offline 1D BPP. The following definition is relatively standard and is arrived at with reference to \cite{munien}, though equivalent normalised versions are also common in the literature \cite{maxence}. 

Let $C$ denote the uniform capacity of each bin, and let $w_{i}$ denote the weight (or equivalently, size) of item $i$, such that $w_{i} \leq C$ for all items. We define $u$ as an upper bound on the minimum number of bins required to pack all $n$ items. We introduce the binary decision variable $y_{j}$, where $y_j = 1$ if the $j$-th bin is opened (used in the solution), and $0$ otherwise, for $1 \leq j \leq u$. Items are labeled $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, and we define a second binary variable $x_{ij}$, which equals $1$ if item $i$ is packed in bin $j$, and $0$ otherwise. The objective function is:
$$ \min\sum_{j=1}^{u} y_j $$ 
This aims to minimise the total number of open bins, subject to the following constraints:
$$ \sum_{j=1}^{u} x_{ij} = 1, \quad \forall i = 1, \dots, n $$
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij} w_{i}  \leq C y_j, \quad \forall j = 1, \dots, u $$
The first constraint ensures that each item is packed into exactly one bin. The second constraint guarantees that the sum of the weights of the items in any given bin does not exceed the bin capacity $C$.

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

The 1D offline BPP is strongly NP-hard, which can be established via a polynomial-time reduction from the 3-Partition problem \cite{garey}. Given an instance of 3-Partition consisting of $3m$ integers $a_1, \dots, a_{3m}$ where $B/4 < a_i < B/2$ for all $i$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{3m} a_i = mB$, we construct a BPP instance with bin capacity $B$ and items of exactly those weights. A valid packing into $m$ bins exists if and only if a solution to 3-Partition exists. Since each $a_i > B/4$, no bin can hold more than three items, and since each $a_i < B/2$, no bin can hold fewer than two; every bin must therefore contain exactly three items summing to $B$. Because 3-Partition is strongly NP-complete \cite{garey}, BPP is strongly NP-hard. As a consequence, no polynomial-time algorithm for solving the BPP to optimality is known, assuming P $\neq$ NP. For large-scale instances, approximation algorithms and stochastic metaheuristics that trade guaranteed optimality for computational efficiency are therefore necessary.

\subsection{Randomised Algorithms}
Randomised algorithms rely on random variable generation to influence their execution paths, making them non-deterministic. In this context, we define stochastic optimisation as the use of randomness to navigate a search space and find near-optimal solutions efficiently \cite{sean}. While strict definitions of stochastic optimisation often require a noisy objective function, the algorithms implemented here primarily use random choices to direct the search process. Therefore, these algorithms largely belong to the subfield of metaheuristics, which are problem-independent frameworks that guide and modify underlying heuristics to escape local optima \cite{yang}. 

These algorithms vary drastically in complexity. The simplest approaches might involve randomising the input order before applying a naive greedy heuristic, which is particularly useful for mitigating worst-case inputs generated by adversaries. Conversely, more complex metaheuristics draw inspiration from biological evolution and natural systems. A primary goal of this paper is to benchmark a diverse variety of these approaches.

\subsection{Large Language Models (LLMs)}
LLMs are trained on massive corpora of text and code, enabling them to capture semantic meaning and structural syntax across both natural and programming languages. This has made them highly capable of generating code, as programming languages share structural similarities with natural text.

Naturally, there are limitations. The code produced by LLMs is not guaranteed to be correct and requires rigorous verification. While models perform exceptionally well on tasks heavily represented in their training data, they often lack the capacity for deep reasoning in novel scenarios \cite{kucharavy}. Consequently, for common tasks (e.g., the Mostly Basic Python Programming dataset), modern LLMs generate reliable solutions. Furthermore, while metaheuristics for the BPP are not an entirely niche domain, the empirical nature of algorithm design poses unique challenges. Specifically, selecting optimal hyper-parameters---such as cooling schedules in Simulated Annealing---and managing the complex interplay between different algorithmic components typically require extensive trial-and-error and human intuition. As these nuances are likely difficult to deduce from the static code found in training corpora, it remains an open question how capable LLMs are at producing performant metaheuristics.

\subsection{Research Questions}
This project aims to compare the empirical performance of human-written randomised algorithms for the 1D offline BPP against implementations generated by state-of-the-art LLMs. Specifically, our key research questions are: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How do various human-implemented randomised algorithms for the BPP compare in empirical performance across diverse problem instances? This establishes a robust baseline and provides insight into practical algorithmic efficiency.
    \item How effective are flagship LLMs at producing functionally correct metaheuristic implementations that achieve competitive solution quality within a fixed time budget?
    \item To what degree does performance vary across different LLMs, and are there identifiable patterns or common algorithmic shortcomings in their generated code?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Traditional Algorithms for the 1D BPP}

The 1D BPP has been studied extensively for over five decades, producing a 
rich landscape of exact, heuristic, and metaheuristic approaches. Exact 
methods, including branch-and-bound and column generation, can solve 
instances to optimality but become computationally intractable as instance 
size grows, reflecting the problem's NP-hardness \cite{garey, maxence}. For 
large-scale instances, practitioners therefore rely on approximation 
algorithms and metaheuristics that trade guaranteed optimality for 
computational efficiency \cite{munien}.

Widely used constructive heuristics include First Fit (FF), Best Fit 
(BF), and their decreasing variants (FFD and BFD), which sort items by 
decreasing weight prior to packing. FFD is particularly well-regarded, 
admitting a tight worst-case guarantee of $\frac{11}{9}\text{OPT} + 
\frac{6}{9}$ \cite{dosa}, and is commonly used as an initialization strategy 
for more sophisticated methods. Beyond these, a broad range of metaheuristics 
has been applied to the BPP. Simulated Annealing approaches have been 
explored by Sonuç et al.\ \cite{sonuc}, who use FFD initialization and a 
sum-of-squares objective to guide the search. Variable Neighbourhood Search 
was explored by Fleszar and Hindi \cite{kryz}, whose VNS implementation 
improves upon the Minimum Bin Slack heuristic in approximately 23\% of cases. 
The Grouping Genetic Algorithm, introduced by Falkenauer \cite{gga} and 
extended by Quiroz-Castellanos et al.\ \cite{quirozca} in the GGA-CGT 
framework, addresses the failure of standard genetic operators on 
grouping problems by encoding bins rather than items as the unit of evolution. 
Ant Colony Optimisation has been adapted for the BPP by Levine and Ducatelle 
\cite{levine}, who assign pheromone between item weights, allowing the colony to learn which weight combinations pack 
efficiently together.

While numerous studies compare subsets of these algorithms, comprehensive 
cross-paradigm empirical comparisons remain rare \cite{munien}. Existing 
surveys tend to focus on algorithmic novelty or theoretical guarantees rather 
than standardised empirical performance across diverse instance classes. This 
makes it difficult for practitioners to assess the relative practical 
effectiveness of different paradigms, and motivates the baseline evaluation 
conducted in Section~\ref{sec:human_eval} of this study.

\subsection{Large Language Models in Code Generation}

LLMs are trained on massive corpora of text and code, enabling them to 
capture semantic meaning and structural syntax across both natural and 
programming languages \cite{chang}. This has made them highly capable of 
generating code, as programming languages share structural regularities with 
natural text. Experiments with models such as GPT-4 have shown that LLMs can 
successfully write greedy algorithms and solve dynamic programming problems, 
occasionally surpassing capable university students in these domains 
\cite{bubeck, chang}. On standard evaluation datasets such as HumanEval and 
the Mostly Basic Python Programming benchmark, modern LLMs generate reliable 
solutions for well-represented tasks. However, LLMs are known to struggle 
with tasks that require genuine reasoning in novel or underrepresented 
scenarios, where pattern-matched knowledge from training data is insufficient 
\cite{kucharavy}.

\subsection{Evaluating LLM Performance on Complex Algorithmic Tasks}

To quantify generative capability beyond standard benchmarks, multiple 
studies have compared human-written code to LLM-generated solutions for 
competitive programming challenges such as those found on LeetCode 
\cite{bubeck, wang, coignion}. These problems exhibit higher logical 
complexity than standard evaluation datasets, providing a closer proxy to 
the difficulty of implementing BPP metaheuristics.

The findings of Coignion et al.\ \cite{coignion} are particularly 
instructive. In terms of execution speed, LLMs frequently outperformed human 
developers, beating 73\% of prior submissions on average. Functional 
correctness was reasonable for well-represented problems, averaging 
approximately 38\% on the most popular LeetCode questions. However, LLM 
performance proved highly sensitive to task novelty: on newly released 
questions absent from training corpora, correctness deteriorated sharply, 
with the strongest model achieving only 9.5\% on a single-attempt basis. 
This suggests that LLM code generation performance conflates knowledge 
retrieval with genuine algorithmic synthesis, a distinction that becomes 
critical when evaluating performance on tasks where subtle design 
decisions --- such as objective function selection and parameter tuning 
--- have an outsized impact on solution quality.

\subsection{LLMs in Metaheuristic Design and Code Generation for Combinatorial Problems}

A growing body of work has begun to examine the use of LLMs for 
automating algorithm design in combinatorial optimisation. A prominent 
strand of this research focuses on using LLMs to generate novel heuristics 
from scratch. ReEvo \cite{reevo} introduces Language Hyper-Heuristics, 
combining evolutionary search with LLM-generated verbal reflections to 
automatically produce and refine heuristic code across multiple 
combinatorial problems, frequently matching or surpassing human-designed 
approaches. This line of work is primarily concerned with algorithm 
innovation rather than evaluation --- the LLM is free to design whatever 
heuristic it determines to be most effective, with no fixed paradigm and 
no human baseline.

A complementary direction uses LLMs to generate complete search algorithms 
for specific combinatorial problems. Rosin \cite{rosin2025} develops a 
protocol in which LLMs freely select and implement heuristic strategies --- 
including simulated annealing and genetic algorithms --- for combinatorial 
design problems, using automated execution feedback from a validity 
verifier to evaluate and select among a large pool of candidate 
implementations. This work demonstrates that LLM-generated code can 
resolve previously open mathematical instances.

The present study contributes to this landscape by evaluating the ability of LLMs  
to implement a specified set of established metaheuristic paradigms from 
scratch, directly comparing the resulting implementations against carefully 
tuned human-written baselines on a well-studied combinatorial optimisation 
problem. Rather than allowing LLMs to freely design or discover algorithms, 
this framing separates two questions that are often conflated: whether 
LLMs can produce performant code, and whether they can faithfully implement 
a given algorithmic paradigm --- including making appropriate design 
decisions such as objective function selection and parameter selection --- 
without the benefit of open-ended exploration.

\section{Human-Implemented Metaheuristics}

To establish a robust empirical baseline, we implemented a diverse suite 
of stochastic algorithms for the 1D BPP. Parameter values were selected through manual trial-and-error tuning on 
a set of uniform instances generated using the same methodology as our 
main evaluation dataset (described in Section~\ref{sec:experimental}), 
hereafter referred to as test-u. This dataset consists of 10 instances 
of 400 items and 10 instances of 800 items. A parameter configuration was considered valid if the 
average execution time across two independent runs rounded to 40 seconds 
or less. Among valid configurations, we selected the 
one achieving the lowest average percentage difference from the theoretical 
lower bound. Where multiple configurations produced similar solution quality, 
we favoured the one expected to generalise better across varying instance 
sizes. The resulting parameters target a maximum average execution time of 
approximately 20 seconds per run, keeping algorithms with steep runtime 
scaling tractable at the larger instance sizes evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}, where 
no time limits are imposed. All algorithms were designed, written, and tuned by the author with 
reference to the relevant literature, with LLMs consulted solely to 
identify bugs and oversights. Corrections were adopted only where the 
fix was consistent with the original implementation intent, with all 
substantive design decisions remaining entirely human-driven.

\subsection{Common Algorithmic Components}

\subsubsection{Lower Bounds}

We first establish the theoretical lower bounds utilised as a strict stopping criterion for the evaluated algorithms. Employing a tight lower bound ensures that algorithms do not waste computational time searching once an optimal solution has been achieved. A simple, well-known lower bound on the number of bins, computable in $O(n)$ time, is given by \cite{martello}:

$$ L_1 = \left\lceil \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}{C} \right\rceil $$

This bound is derived by considering an LP-relaxation of the problem, wherein items are allowed to be fractionally split across bins. In the optimal solution for this relaxation, every open bin is filled entirely to capacity, except potentially the last, which contains the residual weight \cite{fekete}. Despite its simplicity, $L_1$ is highly effective when item weights are small relative to the capacity, guaranteeing a worst-case bound of $\frac{1}{2}$ the optimal solution value \cite{martello, fekete}. However, because the problem instances evaluated in this study feature larger item weights, $L_1$ is often too loose to trigger early termination.

We instead employ the $L_2$ bound developed by Martello and Toth \cite{martello}, which guarantees at worst $\frac{2}{3}$ of the optimal solution value. It requires $O(n \log n)$ time, which is dominated by the initial need to sort the items by decreasing weight. We briefly outline the bound here; for a comprehensive derivation, we direct the reader to \cite{martello}. For any integer $K$ such that $0 \le K \le \frac{1}{2} C$, the items are partitioned into three sets:

$$ \begin{aligned}
I_1 &= \left\{\, i \in \{1,\dots,n\} \;\middle|\; w_i > C - K \,\right\},\\
I_2 &= \left\{\, i \in \{1,\dots,n\} \;\middle|\; C - K \ge w_i > \tfrac{1}{2}C \,\right\},\\
I_3 &= \left\{\, i \in \{1,\dots,n\} \;\middle|\; \tfrac{1}{2}C \ge w_i \ge K \,\right\}.
\end{aligned} $$

Martello and Toth then define the lower bound for a given $K$ as:

$$ L_2(K)=|I_1|+|I_2|+\max\!\left\{0,\left\lceil
\frac{\sum_{i\in I_3}w_i-\left(|I_2|C-\sum_{i\in I_2}w_i\right)}{C}
\right\rceil\right\} $$

This represents a valid bound for every possible value of $K$. The first two terms account for items in $I_1$ and $I_2$, which are strictly greater than half the bin capacity and therefore cannot share a bin with one another. The third term calculates the additional bins required if the total weight of the items in $I_3$ exceeds the combined residual space left in the $I_2$ bins. 

Because the tightness of the bound is dependent on $K$, the strongest lower bound is obtained by maximizing $L_2(K)$ across all valid $K$. To achieve the desired $O(n \log n)$ runtime, the item weights are first sorted in decreasing order, and $K$ is restricted to values that explicitly appear in the item weights. Furthermore, because any item in $I_1$ or $I_2$ is strictly greater than $\frac{1}{2}C$, the combined cardinality $|I_1| + |I_2|$ is invariant with respect to $K$. Our implementation precomputes this constant size once. Then, as we iterate through valid values of $K$, we incrementally update the sums $\sum_{i \in I_2} w_i$ and $\sum_{i \in I_3} w_i$, incrementing the size of $|I_2|$ only as required for the third term. 

Note that while $L_2$ is the bound we use, there is a slightly tighter bound which makes use of dual feasible functions, as developed by Fekete and Schepers \cite{fekete}.

\subsubsection{Fitness Evaluation}

If the objective relied solely on the total number of active bins, it would remain unchanged for the vast majority of transitions — since a move only affects the bin count when it completely empties a bin — leaving the search with no signal to distinguish between neighbouring solutions \cite{gga}.

To circumvent this, we utilise the sum-of-squares objective function, a widely adopted approach originally introduced by Falkenauer for evolutionary bin packing \cite{gga} and subsequently employed across a range of metaheuristic implementations \cite{sonuc, kryz}. We define the fitness of a candidate solution as:

$$ f = \sum_{j=1}^{u} \left( {\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij} w_{i}} \right)^2 $$

This yields a higher fitness score when items are concentrated into tightly packed bins rather than distributed evenly across multiple bins. This formulation guides the algorithm to starve lightly packed bins until they can be emptied completely, thereby reducing the total bin count.

\subsection{Best Fit With Randomised Order}

\subsubsection{Overview}
Best Fit is a classic heuristic typically applied to the online version of the bin packing problem. While rarely used in isolation for the offline variant, where stronger alternatives exist, it provides the simplest possible randomised baseline for comparison. The algorithm operates identically to the standard Best Fit, placing each item into the bin with the tightest available space, but randomises the order of the input items before execution. The purpose of this is to mitigate the impact of adversarial or worst-case input orderings, allowing the heuristic to generally achieve better packings \cite{rajeev}. We refer to this combined procedure as Randomised Best Fit (RBF).

\subsubsection{Implementation}
The algorithm first randomly shuffles the input items, then applies the standard Best Fit heuristic sequentially. For every item, the algorithm scans all currently open bins and places it into the bin that minimises the residual capacity. If an item cannot fit into any existing bin, a new bin is opened. This process continues until all items have been packed.

\subsection{Simulated Annealing (SA)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
Simulated Annealing (SA) is a probabilistic metaheuristic closely related to hill-climbing. A standard hill-climbing algorithm iteratively generates neighboring candidate solutions and transitions to them only if they offer an improvement. SA modifies this approach by introducing a probability of transitioning to a worse solution. This mechanism provides the search trajectory with opportunities to escape local optima, significantly increasing the likelihood of converging on the global optimum. The probability of accepting a deleterious move depends on a temperature value; the higher this is the more likely the algorithm is to move to worse solutions. The temperature decays over time according to a predefined cooling schedule \cite{sean}, \cite{gendreau}.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
The algorithm generates its initial candidate solution using the First-Fit 
Decreasing (FFD) heuristic, mirroring the initialization strategy of Sonuç 
et al.\ \cite{sonuc}. Candidate solution quality is evaluated using the 
sum-of-squares objective function previously defined, with the incremental 
score change normalised by the bin capacity to ensure scale-invariance.

To transition to a neighbouring solution, the algorithm selects two distinct 
bins at random. It then has an equal probability of either moving a single 
random item from the first bin to the second, or swapping two random items 
between them. Infeasible moves, those that would violate a bin's capacity 
constraint, are rejected outright, with no temperature update or acceptance 
calculation performed.

We initialize the temperature $T = 20{,}000$ with an exponential cooling 
schedule of $\alpha = 0.9996$. The algorithm terminates when $T \leq 0.01$, and has no fixed iteration limit.

\subsection{Tabu Search (TS)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
Tabu Search (TS) is a metaheuristic designed to overcome the primary limitation of standard local search methods: getting trapped in local optima. It functions similarly to hill-climbing but incorporates a short-term memory structure known as a tabu list. This list records recently visited configurations, forbidding the algorithm from returning to them for a set number of iterations. By making recent moves tabu, the algorithm is forced to explore novel regions of the search space, allowing it to escape local optima and discover stronger global configurations \cite{sean, gendreau}.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
Similar to our Simulated Annealing approach, the TS algorithm generates an 
initial candidate solution using the First-Fit Decreasing (FFD) heuristic 
and evaluates fitness using the sum-of-squares objective function.

During each iteration, the algorithm explores the localised neighbourhood by 
generating 35 candidate moves. A move consists of selecting two random bins 
and either swapping two items between them or moving a single item from one 
to the other. To aggressively reduce the total bin count, we implement a 
targeted heuristic inspired by Lodi et al.\ \cite{lodi}: when moving a 
single item, there is a 60\% probability that the algorithm will deliberately 
select the least full bin as the source. Because the emptiest bins are the 
easiest to empty entirely, this actively guides the search toward 
configurations that utilise fewer bins.

The algorithm executes for $I = 18{,}500$ iterations, selecting the best 
valid neighbour in each step even if it results in a lower fitness score than 
the current state. To prevent cycling without incurring excessive 
computational overhead, we maintain a tabu list recording the 4 most recent 
moves, storing the configurations of both affected bins for each. Finally, we 
implement a standard aspiration criterion: the tabu status of a move is 
completely ignored if executing that move directly results in the elimination 
of a bin, as this guarantees a strict improvement to the global objective.

\subsection{Variable Neighbourhood Search (VNS)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
Introduced by Mladenović and Hansen \cite{mladenovic}, VNS is a metaheuristic that works by systematically changing neighbourhoods during a perturbation phase (shaking) followed by a local search (often called the descent phase) \cite{gendreau}. 

The algorithm requires defining a set of neighbourhood structures, $N_{k}$, for $1 \le k \le k_{\max}$, where $k_{\max}$ is a user-specified parameter. A neighbourhood $N_{k}$ could, for example, be defined as being $k$ moves away from the current solution. In each iteration of the algorithm, we start with $k = 1$ and continue until $k = k_{\max}$. While $k \le k_{\max}$, we obtain a new candidate solution by "shaking" the incumbent solution, moving from the incumbent to a random solution within its $k$-th neighbourhood. We then apply a local search to this new candidate solution. This is often executed using best improvement (which takes the direction of steepest descent) or first improvement (which selects the first discovered move that improves the solution). 

After the local search, the resulting solution is evaluated using the objective function. If this new solution is strictly better than the incumbent, it becomes the new incumbent, and $k$ is reset to 1. Otherwise, $k$ is incremented to explore a wider neighbourhood. By systematically exploring larger neighbourhoods, the algorithm significantly increases its probability of escaping the current local optimum. As noted in \cite{gendreau}, local minima with respect to one or several $N_{k}$ are relatively close to one another, implying that local optima tend to contain structural information about the global optimum.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
We generate our initial incumbent solution using the FFD heuristic. It 
should be noted that much of our implementation is inspired by the approach 
of Fleszar and Hindi \cite{kryz}, who utilise a different heuristic called 
Minimum Bin Slack (MBS) that generally outperforms FFD \cite{gupta}. 
Fleszar and Hindi's VNS algorithm improves the solution arrived at by their 
$\mathrm{MBS}'$ heuristic in approximately 23\% of cases. We deliberately 
chose to utilise FFD instead of MBS to maintain a more consistent 
initialisation baseline across our algorithms, ensuring that any performance 
differences observed are attributable to the VNS mechanism rather than the 
starting heuristic. Additionally, FFD is more widely used.

Following initialisation, we measure solution fitness using the sum-of-squares 
objective function. To optimise the local search phase, we sort our items by 
weight in non-decreasing order. During the shaking phase, we maintain a list 
of items that have not yet been moved. The algorithm selects an unmoved item 
at random and attempts to move it (either by swapping it with another unmoved 
item or transferring it to a different bin, provided capacity constraints are 
satisfied). Once an item is involved in a move, it is removed from the 
eligible list for the remainder of that shaking step.

After shaking, we execute a best-improvement local search by systematically 
evaluating every possible move across the entire solution space. Specifically, 
for each eligible item (excluding those in completely full bins), the algorithm 
evaluates transferring it to every other open bin, as well as swapping it with 
every other eligible item. The move that yields the greatest improvement is 
selected. To optimise execution, the $\Delta$ fitness improvement is 
calculated by considering only the bins where weights actually change, as the 
squared sum of all other bins remains constant.

Because the items are processed in non-decreasing order of weight, we can 
speed up the search. When iterating through items to transfer into a specific 
bin, the inner loop terminates immediately upon encountering an item whose 
weight exceeds the available residual space.

The algorithm terminates when the theoretical lower bound is reached or after 
a strict limit of 4 iterations. We set $k_{\max} = 3$; although this is 
relatively low, the exhaustive best-improvement local search is computationally 
expensive, and empirical testing demonstrated that larger neighbourhoods 
incurred unreasonable computational costs without a commensurate improvement 
in solution quality.

\subsection{Greedy Randomised Adaptive Search Procedure (GRASP)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
GRASP is a multi-start metaheuristic consisting of two main phases: a constructive phase and a local search phase \cite{gendreau}. These two phases are executed iteratively, and the highest-quality solution found across all iterations is ultimately returned. 

The construction phase generates a candidate solution by iteratively adding components. Rather than acting purely greedily, it selects components at random from a Restricted Candidate List (RCL)—a subset of the remaining components that yield the lowest cost or highest quality. The RCL is typically defined by a threshold quality. For example, if $q^{\min}$ and $q^{\max}$ denote the qualities of the worst and best available components respectively, the threshold is defined as $q^{\min} + (1 - \alpha)(q^{\max} - q^{\min})$, where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ controls the greediness of the search. An $\alpha$ value of 0 results in a purely greedy construction, while an $\alpha$ value of 1 results in a completely random construction. 

Various enhancements can be applied to the standard GRASP framework. In Reactive GRASP, the parameter $\alpha$ is not fixed. Instead, the algorithm initializes with a discrete set of possible $\alpha$ values, each with an equal probability of selection. As the search progresses, these probabilities are periodically updated based on the historical performance of each $\alpha$ value. This feedback loop forces the algorithm to learn from previous iterations and bias the search toward parameters that yield superior solutions. Another notable enhancement is path-relinking, an intensification strategy that explores the trajectory connecting two high-quality solutions found during the search to discover potentially superior intermediate configurations.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
We implemented a Reactive GRASP metaheuristic which runs for 10 iterations. 
During the constructive phase, the quality of an item is defined purely by 
its weight (size). Items are sequentially packed into the currently open bin. 
A new bin is only opened if no unassigned item can fit within the residual 
capacity of the active bin; otherwise, the algorithm restricts its selection 
exclusively to items that can successfully fit.

To govern the RCL, we define a candidate set of $\alpha$ values: 
$\{0.05, 0.10, 0.15\}$. These relatively small values heavily bias the 
construction toward pure greediness, which is highly effective given that 
item weight is a strong heuristic for the Bin Packing Problem (empirical 
testing revealed that $\alpha = 0.05$ consistently yielded the best 
performance). Initially, each candidate value has an equal probability of 
selection. We track the average cost (defined as the total number of bins 
used) of the solutions generated by each $\alpha$.

Every two iterations, provided every $\alpha$ has been utilised at least 
once, the selection probabilities are updated. Let $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, 
\dots, \alpha_m\}$ denote the set of candidate values. The selection 
probability $P(i)$ for the $i$-th value, $\alpha_i$, is calculated as:
$$Q(i) = \frac{N}{\operatorname{avgcost}(i) - \ell \cdot \min_{1 \le j \le m} \operatorname{avgcost}(j)}$$
$$P(i) = \frac{Q(i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{m} Q(j)}$$
where $Q(i)$ represents the relative performance quality of $\alpha_i$, and 
$\operatorname{avgcost}(i)$ is the historical average cost of solutions 
generated using $\alpha_i$. The numerator $N$ represents the total number of 
items being packed, included as a scaling factor for precision.

The parameter $\ell$ serves as a learning rate ($0 \le \ell < 1$) that 
dictates how aggressively the probabilities are altered. Because the absolute 
difference in average bin counts between $\alpha$ values is often exceedingly 
small, $\ell$ is necessary to mathematically amplify these fractional 
differences. We set $\ell = 0.995$, allowing the probabilities to evolve 
organically over time without permitting any single $\alpha$ value to become 
overwhelmingly dominant, which would prematurely stifle exploration of the 
search space. Following the construction of a candidate solution, we execute 
the descent phase. For this, we directly utilise the Tabu Search metaheuristic 
defined previously. To balance intensification with computational efficiency, 
the inner Tabu Search is given 1{,}800 iterations per GRASP cycle.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm (GA)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
The Genetic Algorithm (GA) is a population-based evolutionary metaheuristic inspired by natural selection. A standard GA maintains a population of candidate solutions (chromosomes) and iteratively applies breeding, crossover, and mutation operators to evolve the population toward an optimal solution. 

However, as famously noted by Falkenauer \cite{gga}, standard GAs perform exceptionally poorly on grouping problems like the 1D Bin Packing Problem. In a standard GA, the chromosome encodes information at the item level (e.g., which bin an item belongs to). When standard crossover operators are applied to these item-oriented strings, they completely destroy the carefully optimized structures of well-packed bins, resulting in highly unfit children. 

To resolve this, Falkenauer introduced the Grouping Genetic Algorithm (GGA). The core innovation of the GGA is a paradigm shift in the encoding scheme: rather than focusing on the items, the chromosome encodes the groups (the bins themselves). The length of the chromosome is therefore variable, equaling the number of active bins. This ensures that when crossover and mutation operators are applied, they manipulate, transfer, or delete entire, intact bins rather than scattering individual items.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
While our foundational encoding follows Falkenauer, the majority of our 
genetic operators and heuristics are directly based on the state-of-the-art 
GGA-CGT (Controlled Gene Transmission) framework proposed by 
Quiroz-Castellanos et al.\ \cite{quirozca}. We maintain a population of 16 
chromosomes over a strict limit of 90 generations, with 3 elites preserved 
each generation. Fitness is evaluated using the sum-of-squares objective 
function. To facilitate efficient mutation, bins within each chromosome are 
maintained in descending order of fullness throughout the algorithm.

To initialise the population, we employ the specialised First-Fit 
initialisation strategy detailed in the GGA-CGT framework. The algorithm 
first identifies all items with a weight strictly greater than half of the 
bin capacity. Because these items can never share a bin with one another, 
they are immediately placed into their own distinct bins. The remaining items 
are then randomised and packed using the standard First-Fit heuristic. This 
guarantees a strong, structurally sound starting population.

To transition between generations, we utilise a standard elitist survival 
strategy: the 3 highest-fitness individuals are preserved intact and carried 
directly into the next generation. Six children are then generated via the 
parent selection and crossover mechanism described below. The remaining 7 
slots in the population are filled by applying mutation directly to the 
solutions immediately following the elite set in the fitness ranking.

To generate the 6 children, we implement a parent selection mechanism 
inspired by the Controlled Gene Transmission (CGT) strategy \cite{quirozca}. 
For each required child, a parent pair is formed by drawing one parent 
randomly from the elite set and the second parent randomly from the non-elite 
set. This approach guarantees that high-quality genetic material from the 
elites is consistently transmitted to the offspring, while the non-elite 
parents inject necessary structural diversity to prevent premature 
convergence.

Crossover is applied with a 40\% probability. Our crossover operator sorts 
the bins within both parents in descending order of fullness. It then 
iterates through the parents pairwise, and for each corresponding pair of 
bins inherits them in order of fullness --- the fuller bin first, then the 
other. A bin is skipped entirely if inheriting it would introduce a duplicate 
item already present in a previously inherited bin.

Whether a child is generated via crossover or cloned from a parent, it is 
subjected to mutation. Our mutation operator deletes three bins from the 
chromosome: two selected at random, and one targeted as the least full bin 
in the solution. As bins are maintained in descending order of fullness, 
this is always the final bin in the chromosome. This biases the search toward configurations that utilise 
fewer bins.

Following both crossover and mutation, chromosomes are frequently left with 
unassigned (free) items. To repair these solutions, we apply the rearrangement 
by pairs procedure from the GGA-CGT framework \cite{quirozca}. This procedure 
operates in two distinct stages. First, it iterates through a randomised 
permutation of the active bins and attempts to improve their packing density 
by swapping pairs of packed items with free items. Specifically, it searches 
for opportunities to replace two packed items with either a single heavier 
free item, or a pair of free items that fills the bin more effectively without 
violating capacity constraints. Any packed items removed during this process 
are added to the pool of free items. In the second stage, all remaining free 
items are reinserted into the solution using the First-Fit heuristic. To 
encourage optimal bin filling, these items are sorted in descending order of 
weight if the largest free item exceeds half the bin capacity; otherwise, they 
are processed in a random order.

\subsection{Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO)}

\subsubsection{Overview}
Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by the foraging behaviour of real ants. While initially moving randomly, ants leave behind chemical pheromone trails. Over time, ants travelling along shorter paths return to the nest faster, causing pheromone to accumulate more rapidly on those routes. Subsequent ants probabilistically favour paths with higher pheromone concentrations, creating a positive feedback loop that eventually guides the entire colony toward the shortest path. 

First introduced as Ant System (AS), the framework has seen significant evolution through variants like Elitist AS and MAX-MIN Ant System (MMAS) \cite{stuetzle}, making it highly competitive for complex combinatorial problems \cite{levine}. 

In ACO, artificial ants iteratively construct solutions by selecting components. The probability $p_k(i,j)$ of an ant $k$ transitioning from state $i$ to component $j$ is governed by the following standard equation \cite{aaron}, \cite{gendreau}:
$$p_k(i,j) = \begin{cases} \frac{[\tau(i,j)]^{\alpha}[\eta(i,j)]^{\beta}}{\sum_{c \in C_k(i)} [\tau(i,c)]^{\alpha} [\eta(i,c)]^{\beta}} & \text{if } j \in C_k(i), \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
Here, $\tau(i,j)$ represents the accumulated pheromone (the learned historical quality of the component), and $\eta(i,j)$ represents a heuristic value. The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ control the relative influence of the pheromone and the heuristic, respectively. $C_k(i)$ represents the set of feasible components that ant $k$ can currently add to its solution. Once all ants have constructed complete solutions, the pheromone trails are updated: pheromone is added based on the quality of the solutions found, and a global evaporation parameter, $\rho$, is applied to progressively forget older, suboptimal paths and prevent premature convergence.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
Our implementation was heavily guided by Levine \cite{levine}. To mitigate 
computational overhead and maintain feasible runtimes, we utilise a highly 
constrained search space consisting of just 3 ants executed over 7 iterations. 
Through empirical testing and parameter tuning, we observed that this shortened 
execution window necessitated a heavier reliance on greedy choices over 
historical pheromone accumulation. Consequently, we set the heuristic 
importance quite high at $\beta = 10$, with the pheromone importance at 
$\alpha = 2$ and the evaporation rate at $\rho = 0.2$. The heuristic value 
$\eta$ for an item is defined simply as its weight.

To calculate the aggregate pheromone value for a candidate item $j$ being 
considered for an active bin $b$, we use the adaptation from Levine:
$$\tau_b(j) = \begin{cases} \sum_{i \in b} \tau(i,j) & \text{if } b \neq \varnothing \\ 1 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
This dictates that if item $j$'s weight historically pairs well with the 
weights already present in bin $b$, it will have a higher probability of 
selection. If the active bin is empty (i.e., a new bin has just been opened), 
the pheromone score evaluates to 1, making the initial selection heavily 
dependent on the heuristic $\eta$.

When a solution is completed, the pheromone matrix is updated by first 
multiplying existing values by $\rho$ to simulate evaporation. 
Pheromone is then deposited based on the fitness of the solution, calculated 
using the normalised sum-of-squares objective function established by 
Falkenauer \cite{gga}. The deposited amount is the product of the solution's 
fitness and the frequency of each weight pair within that solution.

To aggressively refine the search space, we incorporate three core concepts 
from the MAX-MIN Ant System (MMAS) introduced by Stützle and Hoos 
\cite{stuetzle} (as Levine did):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Iteration-Best and Global-Best Updates: In each standard iteration, 
    only the iteration-best ant is permitted to deposit pheromone. Furthermore, 
    every $\gamma$ iterations, the global-best ant updates the matrix. We set 
    $\gamma = \lceil 500/n \rceil$, a threshold designed to control the balance 
    between exploration and exploitation within our condensed iteration limit.
    \item Pheromone Trail Limits: To prevent stagnation, pheromone values that fall below $\tau_{\min}$ are reset to the boundary. Following Levine \cite{levine}, no upper bound is enforced on pheromone values, as weight pairs can appear multiple times within a single solution, making a strict $\tau_{\max}$ inapplicable. Instead, $\tau_{\max}$ is used solely to set the optimistic initial pheromone values (see below). Adapting from \cite{stuetzle}, we calculate these values as:
    $$\tau_{\max} = \frac{1}{1-\rho}$$
    $$\tau_{\min} = \frac{\tau_{\max}\left(1 - \sqrt[n]{p_{best}}\right)}{(avg - 1)\sqrt[n]{p_{best}}}$$
    where $p_{best}$ is the probability of constructing the best solution after 
    the trails have converged, set here to 0.05, and $avg$ is $n/2$. It is 
    worth noting these are approximations in the context of the BPP, as 
    the order of items placed within a bin does not alter the final 
    configuration, slightly skewing the mathematical assumptions underpinning 
    the original derivation.
    \item Optimistic Initialization: To encourage broad early exploration, all 
    initial pheromone values are optimistically set to $\tau_{\max}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fitness Dependent Optimizer (FDO)}
\subsubsection{Overview}

Introduced in 2019 by Abdullah and Ahmed \cite{abdullah}, the Fitness
Dependent Optimizer (FDO) is a population-based swarm intelligence
metaheuristic designed for continuous search spaces. While it shares
foundational mathematical similarities with Particle Swarm Optimization
(PSO) --- specifically the concept of updating a search agent's position
by adding a velocity or pace vector --- FDO calculates this $pace$
entirely differently.

Biologically, FDO is inspired by the reproductive swarming process of honey
bees, specifically the behaviour of scout bees searching for a new hive. When
a colony swarms, scout bees explore the environment to identify suitable
targets. Once high-quality candidate hives are found, the scouts communicate
this information to the swarm. When a consensus threshold is reached, the
entire swarm navigates toward the best-identified location.

In the context of FDO, candidate solutions represent the scout bees, and the
globally optimal solution represents the target hive. The algorithm initialises
a population of scout bees that randomly explore the search space. During each
iteration $t$, the position of a search agent $X_i$ is updated according to:
$$X_{i, t+1} = X_{i, t} + pace$$
The $pace$ variable dictates the direction and magnitude of the agent's movement
and is governed by a fitness weight factor $fw$. For minimisation problems,
this is defined as \cite{abdullah}:
$$fw = \left| \frac{f(X^*_{i,t})}{f(X_{i,t})} \right| \times wf$$
where $f(X_{i,t})$ is the fitness of the current agent, $f(X^*_{i,t})$ is the
fitness of the global best solution discovered so far, and $wf \in \{0, 1\}$ is
a user-defined weight factor controlling convergence behaviour. When $wf = 1$
the algorithm exhibits a high level of convergence and a low chance of coverage,
whereas $wf = 0$ effectively removes the fitness weight from the equation
entirely, providing a more stable search. Since $f(X^*_{i,t}) \leq f(X_{i,t})$
for minimisation, $fw$ is bounded in $[0, 1]$, reaching 1 when the current
solution matches the global best.

The value of $fw$ then governs how $pace$ is computed via a branching mechanism.
When $fw \in \{0, 1\}$, the agent has either converged to the global best or
the fitness weight has been disabled, and $pace$ is computed as $X_{i,t} \times r$
for a random number $r \in [-1, 1]$, producing purely random exploration. When
$0 < fw < 1$, the agent's movement is directed relative to the global best
solution. A randomly generated $r \in [-1, 1]$ then determines the direction
of movement: if $r \geq 0$, the agent moves toward the global best according
to $(X_{i,t} - X^*_{i,t}) \times fw$, whereas if $r < 0$, it moves in the
opposite direction via $(X_{i,t} - X^*_{i,t}) \times fw \times (-1)$,
introducing diversification to prevent premature convergence. In both directed
cases, the magnitude of the step is scaled by $fw$, meaning agents with
solutions closer to the global best take proportionally larger steps.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
Our implementation adapts the FDO framework for the discrete 1D Bin
Packing Problem, drawing heavily upon the approach proposed by
Abdul-Minaam et al.\ \cite{abdulminaam}, who first adapted FDO for
this domain. Whereas Abdul-Minaam et al.\ employ a minimisation
objective, we evaluate fitness using the sum-of-squares objective
function, maximised consistently across all other algorithms in this
study. We therefore use the inverse of the minimisation formulation
for $fw$, which preserves $fw \in [0,1]$:
$$fw = \left| \frac{f(X)}{f(X^*)} \right| \times wf$$
where $f(X)$ is the fitness of the current solution and $f(X^*)$ is
the fitness of the global best solution discovered so far. We set
$wf = 1$.

We initialise a population of 20 candidate solutions using the
randomised First-Fit heuristic and maintain a strict execution limit
of 70 iterations, terminating early only if the theoretical lower
bound is achieved.

At the beginning of each iteration, we identify both the global best
solution ($X^*$) and the worst solution in the current population. To
introduce a strong elitist pressure and accelerate convergence, the
worst solution is immediately overwritten by a copy of $X^*$, anchoring
the swarm's search around the most promising known region.

Because the BPP is a combinatorial problem, the continuous position
update operations of the original FDO are adapted into discrete item
transfers. Following Abdul-Minaam et al.\ \cite{abdulminaam}, $pace$
is defined as a sequence of valid item transfers, each described by
the tuple $(\text{item}, \text{current bin}, \text{target bin})$.
Additionally, as the negative movement direction of the original
formulation has no natural equivalent in a discrete combinatorial
domain, only the positive direction is retained. The composition of
the $pace$ sequence is determined by a branching logic based on $fw$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Random Exploration ($fw = 0$ or $fw = 1$): A random threshold
    $r \in [0,1]$ is generated, and a proportion of items equal to $r$
    is randomly selected. Each selected item is transferred to a
    randomly chosen bin distinct from its current one.
    \item Directed Exploitation ($0 < fw < 1$): The value of
    $fw$ itself is used as the threshold proportion, selecting $fw \times
    n$ items. Rather than moving them randomly, each selected item is
    directed toward the specific bin it occupies in the global best
    solution $X^*$.
\end{enumerate}
During the construction of the $pace$ sequence, every proposed transfer is
evaluated against the capacity constraints of the target bin. Invalid
moves are rejected. Once the sequence is finalised, the moves are
executed, the solution's fitness is updated incrementally, and any
resultant empty bins are purged from the chromosome.

\section{Experimental Setup and Methodology \label{sec:experimental}}

\subsection{Instance Generation}
To evaluate the scaling behaviour, computational efficiency, and solution 
quality of our implemented metaheuristics, we generated custom datasets 
inspired by the randomly generated classes established by Delorme, Iori, 
and Martello \cite{BPPLIB}.

Instance sets of sizes $n \in \{100, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1200,
1400, 1600\}$ were generated to observe performance trajectories from
small-scale to large-scale problems. Item weights were bounded relative to the bin capacity $C = 150$: a minimum
weight threshold was uniformly sampled from $[0.1C, 0.3C]$ and a maximum
threshold from $[0.5C, 0.7C]$. The upper bound prevents the generation of
excessively large items that cannot share a bin with any other item, which would trivialise the
packing process. Item weights were then sampled from a uniform
distribution bounded by these instance-specific thresholds and rounded
to the nearest integer.

Because the true optimal solutions for these custom instances are unknown, 
solution quality is evaluated against the $L_2$ lower bound used by each 
algorithm.

\subsection{Execution Environment}
All algorithms were implemented in Python 3.11.2, using native data 
structures to handle bin creation, item assignment, and the structural 
mutations inherent to each metaheuristic. Experiments were conducted on a 
machine equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5825U CPU (1.6GHz base, 4.5GHz 
boost) and 16GB of RAM, running Debian GNU/Linux 12 (Bookworm).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Testing Procedure}
Since the implemented metaheuristics are stochastic, their performance 
can fluctuate between executions. To ensure statistical reliability and 
capture true average-case behaviour, every algorithm was executed 5 
independent times per instance. Performance is reported using two 
metrics.

Execution Time (s) is the mean wall-clock time in seconds across 
the 5 independent runs for a given instance, providing an empirical 
measure of computational efficiency and runtime scaling behaviour.

Percentage Difference (dif\%) quantifies average-case solution quality relative to the theoretical lower bound. For a given instance $i$, the average number of bins used across $R = 5$ runs is:

\begin{equation}
    \bar{S}_i = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} S_{i,r}
\end{equation}

The percentage difference for that instance is then:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Dif}_i = \left( \frac{\bar{S}_i}{L_{2,i}} - 1 \right) \times 100
\end{equation}

To evaluate scaling behaviour, $\text{Dif}_i$ values are averaged across all
instances within each size class. This aggregated metric provides a 
robust, standardised measure of how consistently each algorithm 
approximates the optimal solution across varying problem sizes.

\section{LLM Algorithm Generation Methodology}

\subsection{Model Selection}
To accurately assess the current frontier of automated algorithm design, 
we selected two state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs): OpenAI's 
GPT-5.2 and Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6. At the time of testing (February 
2026), these represented the flagship models from their respective 
organisations and are widely regarded as industry leaders for code 
generation, consistently ranking at the top of comprehensive leaderboards 
such as SWE-bench \cite{swebench}. While focusing on proprietary flagship 
models obscures the exact composition of their training data --- unlike 
open-source alternatives such as StarCoder2 \cite{starcoder2} --- it 
aligns with our primary goal of evaluating the current practical capability 
of AI tools for operations research tasks.

We initially planned to include Google's Gemini 3 Pro in our evaluation 
suite. However, because its highest-tier code generation capabilities 
inherently enforce continuous reasoning tokens that drastically increase 
API costs, conducting the full iterative testing pipeline across all 
algorithms became financially prohibitive, and it was subsequently excluded 
from the final comparison.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering Strategy}
All interactions with the selected LLMs were conducted programmatically 
via their respective APIs. While recent models exhibit less sensitivity to 
prompt phrasing \cite{wangz}, systematic prompt engineering remains 
important.

We employed a zero-shot prompting strategy, meaning no prior examples of 
Bin Packing metaheuristics were provided. This simulates the most natural 
human-computer interaction pattern and avoids biasing the LLM toward 
specific implementation styles. Furthermore, literature suggests that 
zero-shot prompting can sometimes outperform few-shot prompting in advanced 
models, as the LLM focuses purely on the task constraints rather than 
overfitting to the narrative style of the provided examples \cite{reynolds}.

To maximise reasoning quality, we integrated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) 
prompting by appending the phrase ``Let's think step by step'' to the 
instructions \cite{kojima2022}. We utilise this technique because CoT has 
been shown to yield clear improvements over direct prompting specifically 
for code generation tasks \cite{li2024structured}. It is worth noting that 
while Li et al.\ \cite{li2024structured} also introduce a more advanced 
structured approach to CoT, that specific method requires providing 
input-output examples and explicit test requirements, making it 
inapplicable to our zero-shot pipeline. Therefore, we rely on the baseline 
CoT prompt.

Additionally, we utilised persona adoption, instructing the LLM to act as 
a ``senior algorithm designer''. While role-playing has been shown to 
potentially improve reasoning ability \cite{roleplay}, the evidence for 
its effect on code generation specifically is limited. Since there is also 
no evidence suggesting it degrades performance \cite{khojah2025}, we 
included it as a standard practice. Furthermore, to aid the models in 
arriving at correct executables, the required Python function signature 
was provided in the prompt. This has been demonstrated to decrease the 
probability of encountering ImportErrors and TypeErrors in generated code 
\cite{khojah2025}. Finally, the generation temperature for both models was 
left at their default values, as temperature primarily correlates with 
incoherence rather than algorithmic novelty \cite{peeperkorn}.

\subsection{Iterative Generation and Refinement Pipeline}
To move beyond naive single-prompt generation, we implemented an automated 
iterative refinement pipeline inspired by the PerfCodeGen framework 
\cite{perfcodegen}. For every target algorithm, each LLM was allocated 
$k = 3$ independent candidate runs. This number was chosen to balance sample diversity against prompt budget: 
fewer runs would provide too small a sample of candidate implementations, 
while more runs would leave each individual run with an extremely limited 
refinement budget within the fixed prompt allocation. To manage API costs, each run has a 
prompt budget of $p = 5$.

The pipeline for a single candidate run operates in two distinct phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Correctness Loop: The initial zero-shot prompt requests the 
    complete Python algorithm. To ensure a fair comparison and maintain reproducibility, we utilised a standardised prompt template across all LLMs, dynamically substituting only the target algorithm's name into the prompt. The generated code is extracted and executed 
    against a validation suite. If the code throws an execution error or 
    violates constraints (e.g., returning invalid data types), the error 
    traceback is fed back to the LLM in a subsequent prompt with 
    instructions to fix the bug. The algorithm must also abide by a provided time limit (in seconds), with time management handled internally within the implementation itself rather than by external termination. If the execution exceeds this allocated time, the generation is classed as incorrect, and a timeout error is fed back to the LLM for correction. This loop repeats until the code executes successfully within all constraints or the prompt budget $p$ is exhausted.
    \item Performance Loop: Once a syntactically correct and executable 
    algorithm is generated, its baseline performance is evaluated on our 
    standard parameter-tuning dataset (test-u). The algorithm is 
    constrained by a time limit of $\frac{1}{600}$th of a second per item. 
    Given the composition of the dataset, this equates to a 20-second 
    cumulative time limit across all 20 instances. To provide a slight 
    safety measure against anomalously lucky runs without adding excessive 
    computational overhead, this evaluation is repeated twice. The 
    performance metric we optimise is the Percentage Difference (dif\%) 
    from the theoretical lower bound across the instances, ensuring the 
    selection process specifically favours the code variant that 
    consistently approximates the optimal solution. The subsequent 
    refinement steps depend on the remaining prompt budget. If only one 
    prompt remains, the LLM is provided the code alongside its performance 
    results and asked directly to improve it. If two or more prompts 
    remain, the first refinement iteration is split across two prompts: 
    one asking the LLM to generate a strategic refinement plan based on 
    the performance data, and a second asking it to apply that specific 
    plan. For all subsequent iterations in the loop, this process is 
    condensed: we provide the latest performance feedback and ask the LLM 
    to improve the code.
\end{enumerate}
Throughout the performance loop, newly generated code is continually 
validated for correctness. If an optimisation attempt breaks the code, 
the pipeline discards the broken version and reverts to the most 
performant correct version for the next prompt. At the conclusion of the 
$k = 3$ candidate runs, the single highest-performing correct algorithm 
across all runs is selected as the LLM's final submission for that 
specific metaheuristic.

\section{Evaluation of LLM-Generated Algorithms \label{sec:llm-eval}}
All algorithms are evaluated under a fixed wall-clock time budget of 
$\frac{1}{600}$th of a second per item, applied independently to each instance. 
A time-based stopping criterion was adopted over a fixed iteration count because 
per-iteration computational cost varies substantially between implementations of 
the same algorithm --- the human designed VNS uses an exhaustive best-improvement local 
search while LLM equivalents employ lighter-weight search procedures --- and 
across paradigms more broadly, making iteration counts an unreliable proxy for 
compute consumed. By allocating identical time budgets, observed performance 
differences can be attributed to algorithmic quality rather than compute. 
Human-implemented algorithms were adapted to use the time budget---incorporating 
a small safety buffer to guarantee the strict limit was not exceeded---as their 
primary stopping criterion. LLM-generated implementations utilise the great 
majority if not all of it without any adaptation.

To evaluate the generative capabilities of the selected models, we tested the 
LLM-generated algorithms against standard benchmark instances from BPPLIB 
(Falkenauer U and Scholl). Because the optima are known for these datasets, the 
dif\% metric is calculated exactly as before but uses the known optimal rather 
than the theoretical lower bound. The new opt.\ metric denotes the total number of instances where an algorithm 
discovered the optimal solution in at least one of its five independent runs. 
The weighted average dif\% reported in each table is weighted by the number 
of instances in each dataset class. Randomised Best Fit is excluded from this 
comparison, as its trivial structure leaves no meaningful implementation design 
space --- any correct implementation produces equivalent results.

To verify stability, the 5 individual runs comprising each reported average were compared separately; the per-run scores produced consistent rankings across all algorithms, with the sole exception of the Genetic Algorithm where human and Anthropic implementations produced effectively equivalent results.

discovering 1148 optimal solutions compared to 976 and reducing the weighted average

an item from one bin to another gains exactly as much slack in the source bin as it 
loses in the destination. Consequently, the objective function returns an identical 
value for the vast majority of moves and fails to guide the search.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Human-Implemented Algorithms}

The results from Section~\ref{sec:human_eval} provide a clear answer to the first research question. GGA, VNS, and SA form a strong top tier, with average deviations of 1.53\%, 1.71\%, and 1.76\% respectively, though their relative performance is scale-dependent as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:human_eval}. SA offers the most favourable balance of quality and computational efficiency given its near-flat runtime profile. TS, GRASP, and ACO form a consistent mid-tier, with ACO exhibiting steeper runtime scaling than the other two. FDO and RBF are consistently the weakest performers. RBF's poor performance is expected given its single-pass nature, while FDO's weakness is likely attributable to the inherent difficulty of adapting its continuous velocity mechanism to a discrete combinatorial domain, rather than any computational constraint.

\subsection{LLM-Generated Algorithms}

The results in Section~\ref{sec:llm-eval} provide a broadly positive answer to the second research 
question. Both LLMs produced functionally correct code for six of the seven 
algorithms, deviating from the specified paradigm only on FDO, and both frequently matched or exceeded human-level performance.
Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 achieved the strongest result on four of the seven 
algorithms, with the human implementation outperforming both LLMs on GGA and VNS, 
and OpenAI achieving the best result on ACO. The Anthropic SA implementation is 
especially notable: rather than using a fixed cooling parameter, it adopts a dynamic 
schedule that adapts to the available time budget, producing a more effective 
implementation than the human's fixed-parameter approach. Similarly, the Anthropic TS 
implementation uses an iterative bin-reduction formulation rather than conventional 
feasibility-preserving search, which proved substantially more effective on the 
benchmark instances. In both cases the human implementation did not employ these 
approaches, and the performance gap reflects this difference in design.

However, the results also reveal clear limitations. OpenAI's SA implementation 
contained a fundamental flaw in its objective function that rendered the search almost 
entirely uninformative, and its TS implementation underperformed the human 
implementation across nearly all instance classes, most plausibly due to its 
bin-targeting strategy distributing attention across the lightest third of bins rather 
than focusing sustained pressure on a single emptiest bin. These failures were not 
syntactic --- the code executed correctly and produced valid packings --- but reflect 
deeper reasoning errors in algorithm design. This distinction is important: correctness 
and performance are separable, and the iterative pipeline's correctness loop cannot 
detect the latter.

\subsection{Patterns Across LLMs}

Addressing the third research question, the two models exhibit distinct strengths and 
weaknesses. Anthropic performs more consistently across the full suite, producing 
competitive implementations for every algorithm. OpenAI shows higher variance: it 
achieves the strongest result on ACO, where construction-level decisions appear to 
play to its strengths, but struggles considerably on SA where understanding of the objective function and search guidance is critical.

Both models deviated from the specified paradigm on FDO, with neither producing a 
faithful velocity-based translation. This is perhaps unsurprising given that FDO is a 
relatively recent and sparsely documented algorithm whose discrete adaptation for the 
BPP has limited prior work. In this context, the models likely defaulted to more 
familiar and well-represented approaches rather than attempting a genuine FDO 
translation. Whether this constitutes a failure depends on how the task is framed: if 
the goal is to produce the best-performing algorithm, paradigm deviation may be 
acceptable or even desirable; if the goal is to evaluate a specific algorithm's 
generative capability, it undermines the comparison.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgement. First, the human baselines benefited from manual parameter tuning without a fixed budget, whereas the LLMs were constrained to a prompt budget of $p=5$ per run. Furthermore, during the refinement phase, the models only received the most recent working code and its performance metrics, lacking visibility into previously attempted or discarded approaches. The human implementations also prioritised breadth over depth, omitting certain techniques from the reference literature (e.g., the Minimum Bin Slack heuristic in VNS) that could have improved individual algorithm performance. Second, only two LLMs were
evaluated. The planned inclusion of Google's Gemini 3 Pro was excluded on cost grounds,
and reasoning-augmented model variants were similarly excluded, both of which restrict
the generalisability of conclusions about inter-model differences and may have produced
meaningfully different results, particularly for algorithms where subtle design choices
have an outsized impact on performance. Third, all implementations were written in
Python, which imposes computational constraints that disproportionately affect algorithms such as ACO and FDO. A compiled language implementation
might alter the relative rankings of these algorithms. Fourth, the BPP is
a well-studied problem with a substantial body of published implementations, meaning
the models may have had direct or indirect exposure to relevant design patterns during
training. This makes it difficult to distinguish genuine algorithmic reasoning from
retrieval of memorised solutions, and strong LLM performance should be interpreted with
this caveat in mind. Fifth, the LLM generation pipeline is itself stochastic ---
running it again would likely produce different implementations, and the results reflect
one particular instantiation of each model's generative capability. Finally, the initial prompts expressed a preference for fixed-iteration
implementations with a time budget as a secondary stopping criterion, rather
than specifying time-based termination directly; many of the generated
implementations nonetheless adopted time-based stopping as their primary
criterion, and a cleaner design would have specified this from the outset to
ensure consistency across all implementations.

\subsection{Future Work}
Several directions present themselves as natural extensions of this study. The most
direct way to address the training data contamination concern would be to evaluate
LLM-generated metaheuristics on less well-studied combinatorial problems, where
memorised design patterns are less likely to be available, providing a stronger test of
genuine algorithmic reasoning ability. A complementary direction would be to evaluate
on more structurally complex problems such as aircrew scheduling, where constructing
feasible solutions is itself non-trivial and requires nuanced design decisions ---
including how to handle infeasible solutions during the search, since strongly
infeasible solutions can carry useful structural information that guides the algorithm
toward feasibility. Including a broader range of LLMs, particularly open-source models
whose training data is known, would also help disentangle knowledge retrieval from
reasoning capability. Evaluating reasoning-augmented model variants would test whether such capabilities meaningfully improve metaheuristic design quality, particularly for algorithms where the human baselines currently outperform LLM implementations. Additionally, enriching the iterative refinement pipeline---for instance, by supplying the LLM with a complete history of successful and discarded approaches rather than just the latest working version---could yield stronger implementations by allowing the model to learn from its own failed attempts. Finally, all implementations in this study were written in
Python, which is a relatively accessible language to implement in. It would be
interesting to evaluate whether the conclusions hold when implementations are required
in a lower-level compiled language, where the implementation complexity is higher and
the gap between a correct and a performant solution is wider.

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated a diverse suite of human-implemented randomised algorithms for
the 1D Bin Packing Problem and compared them against zero-shot implementations
generated by two state-of-the-art LLMs. Across all three research questions, the
results yield clear and practically meaningful findings.

Among the human-implemented algorithms, GGA, VNS, and SA formed a strong top tier,
with GGA achieving the strongest solution quality overall and SA offering the most
favourable balance of quality and computational efficiency. TS, GRASP, and ACO formed
a consistent mid-tier, while FDO and RBF were consistently the weakest performers.

The LLM evaluation produced results that are both impressive and instructive.
Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 achieved the strongest result on four of the seven
algorithms, with the human implementation outperforming both LLMs only on GGA and VNS.
Several Anthropic implementations incorporated design choices the human baseline did
not --- the dynamic cooling schedule in its SA implementation and the iterative
bin-reduction framework in its TS implementation are both well-established techniques
that the human implementation did not employ, and their inclusion contributed
meaningfully to the performance advantage. OpenAI's GPT-5.2 showed higher variance,
achieving the best result on ACO while struggling on TS and SA. The SA objective
function flaw illustrates a category of failure that is particularly difficult to
detect automatically: the code is correct, the search runs, and valid packings are
produced, but the algorithm is not actually doing what it claims to.

Neither model produced a faithful FDO translation, most plausibly because FDO is a
recent and sparsely documented algorithm with limited prior work on discrete
adaptations. In this case, both models defaulted to more familiar approaches, and their
superior performance over the human FDO implementation likely reflects the suitability
of those approaches for the BPP rather than the quality of the FDO translation itself.

Overall, the findings suggest that flagship LLMs are now capable of producing
competitive metaheuristic implementations from scratch for well-studied combinatorial
problems, drawing on a broad knowledge of existing techniques to assemble
implementations that compare favourably with carefully tuned human baselines. However,
they remain capable of subtle but consequential reasoning errors that pass all
correctness checks while undermining the search. For practitioners, this implies that
LLM-generated optimisation code should be treated as a strong starting point requiring
algorithmic review, rather than a finished product.
